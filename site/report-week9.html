Week 9 — The Bottleneck Moved: 216 NARC Runs

Aug 17, 2026 24 puzzles · 3 models · 3 experiments · 216 runs ResearchResults

Six weeks of MARC work converged on one explanation: models read puzzle structure well and colour badly, and that perception gap is what stops them solving. The first full NARC sweep tests that end to end — and contradicts it. Colour is read at 98.7%. Perception is close to solved. Solving is not, and the two turn out to be unrelated. Meanwhile a heuristic with no model at all — copy the previous frame — beats every model we tested.

Explore the full results dashboard →

In Plain Terms

We showed three AIs 24 little comic strips where one panel is hidden, and asked two separate things: copy back the panels you can see, and tell us what the hidden panel looks like. They were almost perfect at copying — one model got every visible panel exactly right, all 68 of them. They were bad at guessing the hidden panel. And here's the part that matters: being good at copying did not make a model good at guessing. The best copier was the worst guesser. So the thing holding these models back isn't their eyesight. It's that they don't know what should stay the same — asked to continue a story, they redraw far more of the picture than the story actually changes.

What Was Run

Every NARC puzzle in the set, against three vision models, through the Week 6/8 three-experiment battery. One canonical rendered image per puzzle, shared by every run. Thinking off (Week 4 measured it as harmful), narrative on, masked-frame sizes on.

Reconstruction is scored on the visible frames only, so it measures reading with no reasoning mixed in. Across 6,126 visible cells:

qwen3.5-122b reconstructed every visible frame of every puzzle, perfectly. Set against Week 3 — 50.4% cell accuracy and zero perfect grids out of 14 on MARC — that is a different regime.

The honest caveat. This is not a like-for-like win over MARC. NARC puzzles here are much smaller: a median of 100 total cells and 4 distinct colours, mostly 5×5 grids, against a MARC median of 405 visible cells on 7×7–15×15 grids. Some of this jump is the task; some of it is simply that there is less to read. What follows does not depend on which — the solving results are measured against these same easy-to-read puzzles.

Colour reads better than shape, not worse. Only 14.2% of mistakes are one real colour swapped for another; the other 85.8% are a cell that should have been background, or should not have been.

Week 4 left an open item — "probe which specific colour pairs the model confuses" — and named two suspects on palette grounds. The confusion matrix settles both. Blue #0074D9 ↔ light blue #ADD8E6: zero occurrences, across 1,689 blue and light-blue cells. Refuted outright. Black ↔ gray is the largest raw confusion (500 cells, 29.4% of all errors) — but black is 61.7% of every cell in the set, so raw counts mislead. Normalised by how often each colour actually occurs:

Blue is the most-misread common colour — and it is lost to black (211 of its 307 errors), not to light blue. That is a foreground/background failure wearing a colour costume. The model is not struggling to tell hues apart; it is struggling to decide where an object ends.

Checking within each model matters: a negative correlation across all pairs could be one odd model rather than a real effect. It isn't — the relationship is absent inside each model too.

The cleanest single fact in this sweep: qwen/qwen3.5-122b reconstructed every visible frame of all 24 puzzles perfectly, and solved 0 of 24. Of the 53 runs across all models that achieved 100% reconstruction, only 3 (5.7%) also produced a correct answer. Perfect eyesight, no answer.

The chain experiment agrees. Handing a model its own transcription and asking it to solve from text changed the median run by 0.0 points — it helped 25 runs, hurt 25, and left 22 unchanged. When perception is already near-perfect, removing the image changes nothing, which is exactly what you would expect if perception were never the constraint.

Finding 4 — All three models lose to a heuristic with no model in it

Week 8 warned that a NARC masked frame is usually a near-copy of the frame before it, and asked for that baseline beside every score. Here it is. Across this set a masked frame is a median 80% identical to its predecessor (range 25–96%; 7 of 21 puzzles are ≥90% identical).

Read that fourth row carefully. The models do worst relative to the baseline exactly where the baseline is strongest — on the puzzles where almost nothing changes, they lose by 11 points. They are not copying and failing to add the twist. They are changing things that should have been left alone.

What the data is really saying: the models over-edit

That suspicion is directly measurable. For each answer, compare it to the previous frame, and compare the true answer to the previous frame. The truth keeps 74.4% of the previous frame's cells. Every model keeps less:

The thesis. These models can see the scene. What they cannot do is hold it still. Asked to produce the next frame of a story, they rewrite a third to a half of the grid when the story only changed a fifth of it. NARC does not primarily test whether a model can read a picture — it tests whether a model can make a minimal, targeted edit to a scene it has already read correctly. That is a different capability, and it is the one that is missing.

A caveat on the last column: 4, 1 and 0 solves are small numbers, and the ordering of over-editing does not track them monotonically. The over-editing measurement itself is solid — 23 observations per model with clear separation — but treat "least over-editing wins" as a hypothesis this sweep suggests, not one it establishes.

Predictions

Stated now, before the experiments that would test them, so they can be wrong in public.

1 · Greyscale re-encoding will not improve NARC solving

The Week 7 Greyscale Lab was built on the premise that colour is the bottleneck. On NARC, colour reads at 98.7% and blue↔light-blue confusion is literally zero. There is almost no headroom for a colour-free encoding to recover. Prediction: swapping in greyscale encodings via the image-override path moves the NARC solve rate by less than the run-to-run noise, and reduces reconstruction accuracy by removing a channel that currently works. Cheap to test — same JSON, same prompts, different image folder.

2 · Scaling the model will not fix solving

The largest model tested (122b) is the best reader in the set and the worst solver — perfect reconstruction, zero solves. Prediction: a larger vision model pushes reconstruction to a ceiling it has already effectively reached and leaves the solve rate near the copy-baseline. If a bigger model does jump on solving while perception stays flat, the over-editing account is wrong and it should be abandoned.

3 · The narrative is doing less than it appears

Every run here had the story text switched on, and the models still lost to a heuristic that reads no text at all. Prediction: the narrative-off ablation changes solve rate by a small margin — because the narrative is not currently being converted into a targeted edit. This is the highest-value next run and it is one flag: --no-narrative. If accuracy does collapse without it, that is the more interesting result and it means the models lean on language far more than the pixels.

4 · NARC needs lower frame-to-frame similarity to discriminate

With 22 of 24 puzzles having a single masked frame that is a median 80% copy of its predecessor, a large part of any NARC score is available without reasoning. Prediction: puzzles authored with more change per step will separate models far better than the current set, which mostly separates them by how much unnecessary damage they do. Every NARC number should carry the copy-baseline beside it — the dashboard now does this automatically.

Where This Leaves the Project

The research question has been stated since Week 3 as does the metaphor/narrative clue help models solve?, and it has been blocked behind a perception problem the whole time. On NARC, that block is gone — perception is good enough that it is no longer the limiting factor. The question is finally askable.

But the answer this sweep points toward is uncomfortable: with the narrative on, all three models still lose to copy-the-previous-frame. Whatever the story is contributing, it is not yet enough to beat doing nothing. The narrative-on/off ablation now measures something real rather than being swamped by perception noise — and that is the next run.
